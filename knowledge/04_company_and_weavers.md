# The business model

- Started in 1978 with two looms and nine artisans in Churu. He visited weavers' homes by bicycle.
- From 1986 he exported directly and removed middlemen, who had been exploiting weavers.
- Weavers work from home in their villages. The company brings raw material to them and pays fairly and on time. In his words, weavers were "paid twice and on time".
- He worked directly with communities that society had shunned, including Dalit and tribal (Adivasi) artisans, despite opposition from his own community and family.
- He spent 13 years (1986-1999) in Gujarat working with tribal artisans, where he was called "Bhaisahab".
- Vertical integration across the whole chain (wool, dyeing, spinning, weaving, finishing, export), studied by C.K. Prahalad.
- Bunkar sakhis ("weaver friends") are women quality and support officers in the villages.
- Craftsmanship magazine (2018) reported about 40,000 people engaged, more than 80% women, sales to around 45 countries and about $20 million in 2017 sales. His own site (around 2020) says over 60 countries and 600 villages.
- A skilled weaver ties about 360 knots an hour. An 8x10 ft rug at 200 knots per square inch takes roughly 3,000 hours. In his words, a 14x14 rug can take three weavers nearly a year.
- Jaipur Living Inc. (Atlanta, USA) is the US company, led by his daughters Asha (CEO) and Archana (COO).

# Initiatives

- Jaipur Rugs Foundation (2004): artisan development, adult literacy, skills, rights and sustainable livelihoods.
- Higher School of Unlearning (2009): professionals learn from grassroots artisans. "Finding yourself through losing yourself."
- Manchaha / Artisan Originals (2010-2012, led by Kavita Chaudhary): weavers design their own rugs from their own imagination. Manchaha rugs have won international design awards, including a German Design Award for weaver Bimla Devi.
- "The Search for the Divine Soul" (2017): his name for the HR department.
- Founder's Mentality work with Bain & Company (from 2010 pro bono consulting; from 2016 founder's-mentality journey).
- Looms in jails: the company set up looms in Jaipur Central Jail and later in the Open Jail, giving inmates such as Vahid Khan a second chance through weaving.

# Weavers he often speaks about

- Prem Devi: a weaver with no formal schooling who later spoke at New York Fashion Week. She became a bunkar sakhi and learned to read and write through the Foundation's adult education.
- Bimla Devi: weaver who won the German Design Award with a Manchaha rug.
- Vahid Khan: a former inmate who asked for a loom in the Open Jail.

# Essay summary: "Why love matters to business? 3 ideas to thrive & grow" (4 Mar 2022)

He describes a mentoring call where he spoke about love and joy in business and was told this was not relevant to early-stage founders. His reply rests on his college-era belief that "business is next to love". His three ideas:
1. Love is a USP. It is a unique human experience that drives most actions, and it is hard to copy because it is authentic.
2. Love is a bankable asset. It builds goodwill and trust, which make risk-taking possible.
3. Love transforms fear into freedom. Without love, fear and self-doubt drive decisions and organisations lose their purpose.
He suggests an experiment: practise love with wisdom for one month and watch how your personal and professional life changes.

# Essay summary: "Illusion of Knowledge: Life Lessons from 40 years in Business" (14 Feb 2022)

He contrasts highly credentialed professionals with grassroots workers. Formal education can create overconfidence that kills curiosity. "Knowledge is what introduces us to the world", but it becomes a barrier when people believe they already know everything. Society treats degrees and famous institutions as status symbols and undervalues fieldwork. Rural artisans often show more curiosity and humility. His example is Prem Devi, an unschooled weaver who spoke at New York Fashion Week. When hiring, he looks for curiosity and innocence over credentials, because skills grow out of a shared commitment.
